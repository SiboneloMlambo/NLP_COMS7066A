\section{Abstract}
{\bf \looseness=-1
Does a classifier separating \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu}, founded by J.\,T.\ Jabavu in 1884, from the missionary \emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} read politics or print? On the isiXhosa Intellectual Traditions corpus (Nov 1884--1888) we train tuned classifiers along a ladder that removes English, self-references, names and spelling conventions, with non-content baselines, matching and noise injection. Raw text is separated with balanced accuracy 0.93, but spelling and transcription features alone reach 0.85. A model on the remaining shared vocabulary keeps 0.81 (0.78 on matched pairs): a source signal survives, but not one attributable to politics alone. A lexicon supports a political difference: Imvo uses about twice Isigidimi's political vocabulary overall, and more in every common year. On earlier Isigidimi years the classifier shows a temporal shift of unisolated cause.
}
\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{Keywords:} historical isiXhosa; shortcut learning; responsible NLP
\end{quote}

\section{Introduction} \label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
\emph{Isigidimi sama-Xosa} (Lovedale, 1870--1888) was published under missionary control. \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} (King William's Town, from November 1884) was founded by John Tengo Jabavu, who had edited Isigidimi from 1881 \citep{sahojabavu} and left because the missionaries were reluctant to publish explicitly political material \citep{odendaal1984vukani}. The digitised archive of \citet{schoots2023isixhosa} makes the split testable: if the break was political, the papers should differ in \emph{what} they discuss. A source classifier looks like natural evidence, but classifiers exploit whatever separates the labels \citep{gururangan2018annotation,geirhos2020shortcut}, and historical text is full of such shortcuts: collection-specific transcription error \citep{hill2019quantifying,vanstrien2020assessing}, mastheads, adverts and house spelling.

\textbf{Research question.} \emph{When a classifier separates the two papers over the years both appeared, how much of the separation is content, and how much is identity, transcription noise and orthography?} We test whether (H1) raw accuracy is inflated by shortcuts, (H2) a signal survives their removal, and (H3) that residual is political rather than religious. Mistaking print for politics would yield false claims about historical Black writers in a low-resource language \citep{joshi2020state}.

\section{Methods}\label{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} We use both papers from November 1884 (Imvo's first issue) to December 1888 (Isigidimi's last; its 1886 volume is lost). Pages are cut into 200-token windows (6{,}699 Imvo and 1{,}509 Isigidimi segments from 211 and 38 issues). Splits are 70/15/15 \emph{by issue}, stratified by paper and year, for three seeds. Validation and test segments sharing over 30\% of their word 6-grams with training text (reprinted adverts) are removed: 558 of 2{,}512, 277 of them from test.

\textbf{Cleaning ladder.} L0 is the full page; L1 keeps only the dataset's isiXhosa paragraphs (Imvo is 27\% English, Isigidimi 1\%; \citealp{schoots2023isixhosa}). L2 also removes self-references (titles, place of printing, staff), digits and punctuation. L3 also masks capitalised non-sentence-initial tokens (names). L4 is lower-cased, equalises orthography (\grapheme{bh}$\to$\grapheme{b}, \grapheme{xh}$\to$\grapheme{x}, no elision apostrophes) and keeps the 3{,}485 words seen $\geq5$ times in \emph{both} papers' training data, so one-paper spellings and OCR errors are unusable.

\textbf{Models.} Word and character $n$-gram TF-IDF ($n$ from 2 up to 4 or 5, tuned) with class-balanced logistic regression, Complement Naive Bayes, and fastText subword embeddings \citep{bojanowski2017enriching} trained on the training split and mean-pooled. Pretrained encoders \citep{alabi2022adapting} could not be downloaded here. Hyper-parameters are grid-searched on validation macro-F1 (scores plateau beyond the grid edge; Supp.\ Table~\ref{tab:s3}), and each test set is used once. With Isigidimi at 18\% of segments, we report macro-F1 and balanced accuracy (BA) with 95\% CIs from a bootstrap over issues.

\textbf{Isolating the signal.} (i) \emph{Feature families} on L4: the 100 most frequent shared words (cf.\ function-word stylometry; \citealp{mosteller1964inference}) versus the remaining shared vocabulary. Frequency separates style from topic only loosely (the top 100 include \emph{abantu}, ``people''), so a sensitivity model also drops 359 grammatical fragments and residual names. On raw text: 15 transcription-quality proxies (e.g.\ vowel-less tokens; not measured OCR error rates, as the archive was human-corrected) and 4 orthographic rates (\grapheme{bh}, \grapheme{xh}, elision, hyphens). (ii) \emph{Matching}: each Isigidimi test segment is paired 1:1 with its nearest Imvo segment on the 19 standardised non-content features, within a median-distance caliper \citep{stuart2010matching}. This gives 134 pairs and cuts the mean absolute standardised difference from 0.29 to 0.08. (iii) OCR-style \emph{noise injection} into Isigidimi test text; (iv) a \emph{temporal hold-out} (test on 1888). (v) \emph{Probe}: the content model scores unseen Isigidimi issues from 1876--80 (before Jabavu) and 1881--Oct 1884 (under Jabavu). (vi) \emph{Lexicon}: 14 political patterns (10 concepts, e.g.\ \emph{palamente} parliament) and 12 religious (11 concepts, e.g.\ \emph{Tixo} God), drafted before comparing the papers and audited by concordance (Supp.).

\section{Results}\label{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Shortcuts inflate accuracy (H1).} On full pages character $n$-grams reach BA $0.93\pm0.01$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}). The strongest L1 character features are house conventions: Imvo writes \grapheme{bh} (8.7 tokens per 1{,}000 vs.\ 0.01) and \grapheme{xh}, while Isigidimi uses elision apostrophes three times as often. Imvo also has 2.4$\times$ more vowel-less tokens, a proxy for residual transcription errors. Non-content features alone reach BA 0.85 (Table~\ref{tab:main}).

\textbf{A source signal survives (H2).} Each control lowers accuracy, yet the L4 word model keeps BA $0.83\pm0.03$ across splits and the remaining vocabulary 0.81 (0.78 without fragments and names). Matching costs this model 0.03 (to 0.78 [0.73, 0.82]) and the non-content models 0.10--0.12 (to 0.75 [0.70, 0.79]); the intervals overlap. Corrupting 5\% of Isigidimi tokens flips 2--5\% of segments to ``Imvo''. Tested on unseen 1888, the L4 model keeps BA 0.80. fastText embeddings (about 1M tokens) trail sparse features.

\textbf{Lexicon results support a political difference (H3).} Top Imvo content features include \emph{abamnyama} and \emph{amawetu} (``black people'', ``our people''), \emph{imantyi} (magistrate), \emph{amatyala} (court cases), and \emph{entolongweni} (in prison). Isigidimi's include \emph{imbali} (history), \emph{inkosi} (chief), \emph{imfazwe} (war) and \emph{Ntsikana}, the early Xhosa Christian hymn-writer. Imvo uses 14.0 political terms per 1{,}000 tokens [13.2, 14.7] against Isigidimi's 7.4 [6.2, 8.7] (religious: 3.2 vs.\ 5.1). In each of the four years both papers appeared, Imvo's rate is higher (by 4.3--7.1; within-year permutation $p<0.001$).  Within Isigidimi (Fig.~\ref{fig:eras}a), the political share rises from 0.54 [0.49, 0.58] in 1876--80 to 0.67 [0.63, 0.70] under Jabavu, then falls to 0.59 [0.54, 0.63] after he left.

\textbf{The classifier is a poor clock.} On unseen Isigidimi years (Fig.~\ref{fig:eras}b) the content model calls 66\% of Jabavu-era segments ``Imvo'', but also 58\% of 1876--80 segments, against 26\% of held-out post-1884 ones. The Jabavu-era excess over 1876--80 is small (+0.05 mean probability, CI [0.03, 0.07]).

\section{Discussion}\label{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
Print alone separates the papers (BA 0.85), so a 0.93 source classifier is no evidence of a political split (H1). After the controls a source signal near 0.8 remains (H2); the lexicon ties part of the difference to political vocabulary (H3), but genre, vocabulary and residual conventions also separate the papers. This fits the account that Jabavu left to write politics freely \citep{odendaal1984vukani}: Isigidimi's political share peaked in his last years there, then fell. Its rise began in the late 1870s, suggesting gradual politicisation rather than a single editor effect.

\textbf{Responsible NLP.} \emph{Ablate before interpreting:} at L1, \grapheme{bh} and apostrophes would pass for Imvo's ``voice''. \emph{Noise is partly culture:} Imvo's spelling (e.g.\ \emph{Xhosa}, never in Isigidimi) is plausibly editorial; removing it suits questions of topic, not identity, and must be documented \citep{bender2018data}. \emph{Forced-choice models misread time:} a binary classifier assigns every unfamiliar text to a class, so Fig.~\ref{fig:eras}b reflects a temporal shift of unisolated cause, not that 1876 Isigidimi ``sounded like'' Imvo; such outputs must not become ideological labels \citep{blodgett2020language}.

\textbf{Limitations.} With 38 Isigidimi issues, CIs are wide; matching cannot remove unmeasured confounds; frequency and genre are confounded with source; and the lexicons, built by a non-first-language speaker, impose a modern politics/religion split on writers for whom the two were entwined.

\section*{Supplementary Material}
\textbf{S1. Implementation details.} Segments are 200 whitespace tokens (windows with fewer than 60 raw or 30 L4 tokens are dropped). Grids: word LR $C\in\{0.3,1,3,10,30\}$, $n$-grams $\{(1,1),(1,2)\}$, min.\ df $\{2,5\}$; char LR $C\in\{1,3,10,30\}$, $n\in\{(2,4),(2,5)\}$; Complement NB $\alpha\in\{0.01,0.03,0.1,0.3,1\}$, min.\ df $\{2,5\}$; fastText (skip-gram, window 5, subwords 3--5, 10 epochs, one worker thread, \texttt{PYTHONHASHSEED=0}, 200k hash buckets) dim $\{50,100\}$ with LR $C\in\{0.01,0.1,1\}$; non-content LR $C\in\{10^{-3},\dots,10^{2}\}$; histogram gradient boosting learning rate $\{0.05,0.1\}$, depth $\{2,3,5\}$. All logistic regressions are class-balanced. Selected values for every run are in \texttt{outputs/results.csv}. Software: scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011scikit}, gensim \citep{rehurek2010software}. Compute: CPU only (2 cores, 8\,GB RAM); about 65 minutes wall-clock with the two README commands run in parallel.

\textbf{S2. Lexicons} (patterns matched inside lower-cased tokens; old orthography; 14 political patterns for 10 concepts, 12 religious patterns for 11 concepts). \emph{Political:} palamente (parliament), voti/vota (vote), nyulo/nyulw/nyula (elect, election; excluding \emph{-qanyul-} ``cut''), rulumen (government), mteto/miteto (law), lungelo (right), rafu (tax), mantyi (magistrate), tyala (court case, charge), koloni (the Colony). \emph{Religious:} tixo (God), yesu (whole word), kristu (Christ), tandaz (pray), zibalo (scripture), vangeli (gospel), ngcwele (holy), sono (sin, word-final), mfundisi/bafundisi (minister/s), cawa (church, Sunday), nkonzo (service). The patterns were drafted from general knowledge of isiXhosa before any per-paper comparison; the concordance audit (Table~\ref{tab:audit}) removed or anchored patterns with false matches (Table~\ref{tab:rejected}), and none was added or removed because of the direction of a difference. \emph{bhunga} (council) was dropped because Isigidimi never writes \grapheme{bh}, so it could only match Imvo's spelling. The glosses are the author's and have not been reviewed by a first-language speaker.

\textbf{S3. Synthetic noise.} The noisier paper was identified by its rate of vowel-less plus mixed letter--digit tokens (Imvo 3.8\% vs.\ Isigidimi 1.4\% of tokens; rare-token rate was not used because it is confounded with each paper's share of the training vocabulary). Confusions were injected into Isigidimi test segments at 0--40\% of tokens. Fig.~\ref{fig:s1} reports per-segment flips: the share of segments whose prediction changes from Isigidimi to Imvo relative to the uncorrupted text.
